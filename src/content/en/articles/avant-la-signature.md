A project does not start on kick-off day. It starts while the tender response is being written, when we decide what to promise, at what price and on what schedule. Whatever goes into the bid then becomes the commitment I manage, sometimes for several years.

That is why I treat pre-sales as the first act of project management, not as a commercial step that comes before it. Here is what I look at.

## Read what the specification leaves out

A specification describes the need as the client sees it at the time of writing. It rarely says everything. Before pricing anything, I look for what is missing, because that is where cost and schedule gaps hide.

- **Operations.** Who will run the application after delivery, with what skills and on what infrastructure.
- **Security accreditation.** Who grants it, on the basis of which documents, and when in the schedule.
- **Data.** Where it comes from, what must be migrated from existing systems, and what happens to it when the contract ends.
- **Dependencies.** Other projects, other suppliers, and the decisions expected from the client itself.

When a question has no answer, I ask it during the clarification period set aside for that purpose. An answer obtained before the bid is submitted is worth more than an assumption found to be wrong in the third month.

## Price security, don't just mention it

In the public sector, security often weighs more on the schedule than development itself. A risk assessment to carry out, an accreditation file to build, staff to get security-cleared, access to premises or systems to obtain: each of these steps takes time, and most of them are outside the project team's control.

A bid that promises security without pricing it pushes the problem into delivery. I would rather see these activities in the bid, with their effort and their place in the schedule. The offer looks slightly more expensive on paper. It is above all more accurate, and the project that follows has a better chance of keeping its dates.

## Write the assumptions down

Every estimate rests on assumptions: a volume of data, a number of users, the availability of key people, an environment provided by a given date. As long as they stay implicit, everyone reads them their own way.

So I write them into the bid, as precisely as I can. They serve twice. During selection, they let the client compare offers on the same basis. During the project, they make clear what is covered by the agreed price and what is not.

When an assumption turns out to be wrong, a contract amendment is the planned way to deal with it. It is not a project failure. It is the result of a carefully written commitment, and it allows adjustment without conflict.

## Have the bid reviewed by the people who will deliver it

A bid written without the team that will carry it out creates a gap from day one. I have the effort and the schedule reviewed by the people who will lead development, architecture and security. They see what the writer cannot: an integration harder than announced, a technology nobody on the team knows yet, a sequence that cannot work.

This review has another effect. The team that starts the project already knows its commitments, and has agreed to them.

## Say what will not be done

What is out of scope matters as much as what is in scope. I list in the bid what is not included: data migration beyond a certain volume, support after a given date, integration with a system that has not yet been defined. These lines prevent misunderstandings that would otherwise be settled later, at a higher cost.

## What a decision-maker can look for in a bid

On the buyer's side, these principles give a few simple markers for reading a proposal.

- Are the assumptions written down, and are they realistic?
- Do security and accreditation activities have an effort and a place in the schedule?
- Are the main risks named, with a way of handling them?
- Is the out-of-scope list explicit?
- Is it clear who in the proposed team will lead the project day to day?

A bid that answers these questions clearly is not necessarily the cheapest. It is often the one that costs least in the end.
